06

The complete reference

One hundred lessons, edited into an operational index. Search by behaviour, setting, or decision.

100 lessons

01What reading people means

Estimate behaviour, emotion, motives, incentives, beliefs, values, capacity, and reliability separately. One label cannot hold a person.

02The core model

Read behaviour as a product of person, situation, incentives, current state, relationship, and history.

03Five levels of evidence

A single cue is weak. Repeated behaviour under meaningful cost gives the strongest evidence of character.

04Baseline before interpretation

Learn what is normal for this person. Change from baseline carries more information than the behaviour alone.

05Context before psychology

Ask what the role, culture, room, or immediate pressure explains before inferring a trait.

06Actions before words

Track where time, attention, money, and sacrifice go. Discrepancies may reveal competing values rather than dishonesty.

07Follow the incentives

Study what the environment rewards and punishes. Preference and incentive pressure often act together.

08What they protect

Disproportionate defensiveness may mark identity investment in status, competence, morality, or belonging.

09What they brag about

Repeated boasting shows what someone values and wants others to value in them.

10What they envy

Repeated, intense criticism can point to aspiration, threat, or identity conflict. It does not prove projection.

11What they admire

Ask who they respect and why. The chosen adjective often reveals an aspirational value.

12What they fear losing

The same pursuit can express desire or fear. Ask what fails if the person’s current strategy stops working.

13Repeated trade-offs

Values become visible when they conflict. Watch what repeatedly wins against money, approval, health, or autonomy.

14Reading emotions

Record changes in speech, tension, timing, humour, and topic. Keep several emotional explanations alive.

15Facial expressions

A facial change after a specific stimulus tells you where to investigate. It does not tell you the cause.

16Eye contact

Culture, neurotype, power, attraction, and habit all affect gaze. Topic-specific change is more useful than low eye contact.

17Listen to the voice

Pace, pitch, breathing, word choice, and sentence length may expose uncertainty without proving deception.

18Response latency

A pause means the question required more processing. Thought, emotion, uncertainty, and filtering can all cause it.

19Watch pronouns

Repeated shifts between I, we, and they may reveal ownership, distance, or identification. Do not psychoanalyse one pronoun.

20Ask for specifics

People with direct experience can usually move between a clear summary and a concrete example.

21Compare audiences

Notice how someone treats the boss, intern, waiter, outsider, and people they do not need.

22Power behaviour

Authority lowers the cost of expressing existing tendencies. Watch credit, dissent, responsibility, information, and protection.

23Stress behaviour

Pressure reveals default regulation: control, withdrawal, blame, humour, analysis, reassurance, or collaboration.

24After failure

Watch whether the person denies, blames, repairs, seeks feedback, or changes method.

25After success

Success reveals how someone handles credit, risk, loyalty, generosity, status, and growing demands.

26Apologies

A useful apology names the act and impact, accepts ownership, repairs harm, and changes later behaviour.

27Boundaries

A polite no tests entitlement. Acceptance differs from pressure, guilt, mockery, punishment, or endless negotiation.

28Reciprocity

Track who initiates, listens, accommodates, remembers, sacrifices, and repairs. Mutuality need not mean identical effort.

29Curiosity

Genuine interest produces questions, memory, and a model of you that changes as you answer.

30Gossip

How someone handles another person’s private information reveals values, jealousy, status awareness, and confidentiality norms.

31Humour

Repeated targets reveal warmth, aggression, insecurity, and status-testing. Punching down deserves attention.

32Status behaviour

Notice whether warmth rises after someone learns a title, school, income, connection, or source of access.

33Competence and confidence

Judge competence through specificity, track record, explanation, prediction, decisions, and error correction.

34Confidence and dominance

Conversation control may come from anxiety or status sensitivity. Confidence can tolerate silence, questions, and changed views.

35Kindness and agreeableness

A kind person may disagree. Kindness shows when the right action requires discomfort.

36Niceness and trust

Warmth and charm do not prove honesty, discretion, follow-through, or stability when incentives change.

37Intelligence and wisdom

Clever explanation differs from sound judgement about second-order effects, costs, and human consequences.

38Ambition

Ask what ambition seeks: mastery, security, status, power, freedom, impact, recognition, revenge, or family uplift.

39Loyalty

Loyalty becomes visible when it costs time, money, status, or ease. It need not excuse abuse or incompetence.

40Integrity

Integrity appears when someone keeps a stated principle while violating it would bring a real advantage.

41Manipulation

Look for repeated guilt, urgency, confusion, isolation, selective disclosure, and shifting goals that weaken choice.

42Flattery

Check whether praise is intense, attached to a request, aimed upward, or withdrawn after refusal.

43Too much, too soon

Fast intimacy and commitment reduce observation time. Increase the observation window as the stakes rise.

44Romantic interest

Use clusters: reciprocal initiation, time, memory, disclosure, effort, proximity, and consistent engagement.

45Professional interest

Follow-up, specific questions, introductions, resources, and scheduled next steps outweigh polite enthusiasm.

46Negotiators

Track anchors, concessions, silence, time pressure, alternatives, constraints, and conditional language before inferring personality.

47Leaders

Judge decisions, information flow, incentives, talent growth, ownership, and whether bad news travels safely.

48Teams

Map interruption, attention, translation, expertise, blame, protection, formal authority, and informal authority.

49Read the room

Observe before joining. Find the formal lead, informal lead, connector, expert, outsider, performer, and quiet influence.

50Who looks at whom?

After uncertainty, conflict, or a risky joke, watch whose reaction the group checks first.

51Seating and proximity

Position may suggest comfort, alliance, or hierarchy. Room design and culture keep this signal weak.

52Silence

Silence can mean thought, fear, respect, disagreement, ignorance, or disinterest. Read what surrounds it.

53Incongruence

When words, voice, and face disagree, investigate. Politeness, fatigue, restraint, and resentment remain possible.

54Ask about reservations

Name the mismatch without accusation: “It sounds as if you have reservations. What concerns you most?”

55Lie detection

No universal gesture proves a lie. Compare accounts, request specifics, verify independently, and track contradictions.

56Bayesian updating

Start with several plausible causes. Raise or lower their probability as new behaviour arrives.

57Competing hypotheses

Force at least three explanations and ask which future evidence would separate them.

58The three-hypothesis rule

Write your first reading, a benign alternative, and a different mechanism. Then observe.

59State and trait

A state belongs to this moment or topic. A trait needs repetition across contexts.

60Capacity and character

Separate whether someone can deliver, whether they will, and whether trusting them is wise.

61Intent and impact

Good intent can coexist with repeated harm. Sincerity alone does not make someone safe to rely on.

62Explanation and excuse

An explanation names a contributing factor. Mature ownership keeps responsibility in view.

63Confidence levels

Use low confidence for one cue, moderate for clusters, and high for repetition across contexts.

64Pure observation

For two weeks, record visible and audible facts without emotional or personality labels.

65Baseline building

Learn familiar people’s normal speech, gaze, humour, posture, energy, disclosure, and question frequency.

66Prediction

Predict a small next action before the interaction ends. This turns intuition into a scoreable claim.

67Prediction journal

Record the situation, prediction, confidence, outcome, and reason for the hit or miss.

68Motive mapping

List what the person may want, fear, protect, gain, and change. Keep several answers.

69Ask better questions

Ask what shaped the choice, felt hard, mattered, or could have changed the decision.

70Elicit stories

Ask for a recent decision or disagreement. Behavioural stories reveal more than abstract self-description.

71The last-time question

When someone names a trait, ask when it last appeared under real conditions.

72Watch groups

Meetings and gatherings expose initiative, followership, interruption, repair, deference, and topic control.

73Compare contexts

Observe the same person at work, socially, under stress, after success, and across hierarchy.

74Study your biases

Audit halo, confirmation, attribution, projection, status, similarity, and negativity bias in your own readings.

75Calibrate through feedback

When appropriate, ask whether your reading matched their experience. A wrong answer improves the model.

76The 30-day programme

Move from observation to baselines, prediction, competing explanations, questions, group dynamics, and error review.

77The daily drill

After one interaction, record facts, baseline change, three causes, incentives, a prediction, and confidence.

78Movies as practice

Predict status, relationship, and emotion with sound off. Remember that scripted scenes are unusually legible.

79Interviews as practice

Pause before answers. Predict the response, then watch specificity, revision, challenge, and accounts of failure.

80Negotiations as practice

After meetings, identify each party’s needs, symbols, alternatives, constraints, and time pressure.

81Children as practice

Observe attention, competition, fairness, sharing, and frustration without assigning adult motives.

82Read yourself

Track your defensiveness, status seeking, interruptions, concealment, overexplaining, and speech under anxiety.

83The empathy upgrade

Ask which beliefs make the behaviour intelligible. Understanding a mechanism does not require agreement.

84Perspective-taking

Build the strongest version of another person’s beliefs, evidence, fears, and target outcome. Predict from that model.

85The game-theory layer

Map players, choices, payoffs, information, sequence, and exit before blaming personality.

86A difficult boss

Replace diagnosis with observed patterns, clear options, written records, and fewer verbal dependencies.

87A romantic partner

Separate reduced interest, overload, and different communication needs. Predict, then talk directly.

88A business partner

Repeated missed estimates establish low forecasting reliability. Plans should stop depending on optimistic promises.

89Skip the total theory

Answer the decision question: confidentiality, delivery, disagreement, boundaries, alignment, hiring, or dependence.

90Decision-relevant depth

Scale the evidence threshold with the stakes. Coffee, marriage, hiring, and asset control require different depths.

91The trust ladder

Move from conversation to commitments, responsibility, dependence, and high-stakes trust as reliability appears.

92Use systems

Written agreements, small trials, references, boundaries, and independent checks reduce reliance on perfect intuition.

93Use intuition as a lead

Translate “something is off” into an exact cue, a hypothesis, and a test.

94Guard against overreading

Elegant psychological stories deserve more evidence. Sometimes the person was tired.

95Read structure

Use patterns over time, incentives, trade-offs, pressure, resources, and relationships as your strongest evidence.

96Add compassion

Ask what the behaviour predicts and what makes it understandable. Combine accuracy, empathy, and boundaries.

97The master algorithm

Observe, add context and baseline, find change, map incentives, generate explanations, predict, verify, and update.

98Twelve dimensions

Estimate motivation, fear, identity, values, incentives, trust, stress, power, failure, success, relationships, and growth.

99Predict behaviour

Understand enough about patterns and likely responses to choose an intelligent action. A total theory is unnecessary.

100The training doctrine

Move from observation to pattern, hypothesis, prediction, feedback, and calibration. Keep unknowns visible.

State what you observed. Keep three explanations alive, test their predictions, and match trust to the evidence.
