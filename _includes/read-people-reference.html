06

One hundred field notes

Search by behaviour, setting, or decision.

100 lessons

01What reading people means

Think about behaviour, emotion, motives, rewards, beliefs, values, ability, and reliability separately. One label cannot describe a whole person.

02A useful model

Behaviour comes from the person, the situation, what they stand to gain or lose, how they feel now, their relationship with you, and their history.

03Five levels of evidence

A single cue is weak. Repeated behaviour under meaningful cost gives the strongest evidence of character.

04Learn what is normal first

Learn what is normal for this person. A change from their usual behaviour tells you more than the behaviour alone.

05Check the situation first

Before you judge their character, ask what the role, culture, room, or immediate pressure could explain.

06Actions before words

Notice how they spend time, attention, and money, and what they give up. A gap between words and actions may show two values in conflict, not dishonesty.

07Follow the rewards

Study what the setting rewards and punishes. What people prefer and what they are rewarded for often work together.

08What they protect

An unusually defensive reaction may show that their status, skill, morality, or sense of belonging feels threatened.

09What they brag about

Repeated boasting shows what someone values and wants others to value in them.

10What they envy

Repeated, intense criticism can point to desire, threat, or conflict about who they want to be. It does not prove that the criticism is really about themselves.

11What they admire

Ask who they respect and why. The word they choose often shows a quality they want for themselves.

12What they fear losing

The same pursuit can express desire or fear. Ask what fails if the person’s current strategy stops working.

13Repeated trade-offs

Values become visible when they conflict. Watch what repeatedly wins against money, approval, health, or freedom.

14Reading emotions

Record changes in speech, tension, timing, humour, and topic. Keep several possible emotions in mind.

15Facial expressions

A facial change after a specific event tells you what to ask about. It does not tell you the cause.

16Eye contact

Culture, brain differences, power, attraction, and habit all affect eye contact. A change around one topic tells you more than low eye contact by itself.

17Listen to the voice

Pace, pitch, breathing, word choice, and sentence length may show doubt. They do not prove a lie.

18Time before answering

A pause means the question needed more thought. Thinking, emotion, doubt, and choosing words carefully can all cause it.

19Watch pronouns

Repeated shifts between I, we, and they may show ownership, distance, or belonging. Do not read too much into one pronoun.

20Ask for specifics

People with direct experience can usually move between a clear summary and a concrete example.

21Compare audiences

Notice how someone treats the boss, intern, waiter, outsider, and people they do not need.

22Behaviour with power

Power makes it easier to show habits that were already there. Watch how someone handles credit, disagreement, responsibility, information, and protection.

23Behaviour under stress

Pressure shows how someone usually copes: they may control, withdraw, blame, joke, analyse, reassure, or work with others.

24After failure

Watch whether the person denies, blames, repairs, seeks feedback, or changes method.

25After success

Success reveals how someone handles credit, risk, loyalty, generosity, status, and growing demands.

26Apologies

A useful apology names what happened and who it hurt, takes responsibility, repairs the harm, and leads to changed behaviour.

27Boundaries

A polite no shows whether someone feels entitled to your yes. Acceptance looks different from pressure, guilt, mockery, punishment, or endless negotiation.

28Give and take

Track who starts contact, listens, adjusts, remembers, gives things up, and repairs harm. Both people do not need to make the exact same effort.

29Curiosity

Real interest leads to questions. The person remembers your answers and changes how they understand you.

30Gossip

How someone handles another person’s private information shows their values, jealousy, concern with status, and respect for privacy.

31Humour

Who someone jokes about can show warmth, aggression, insecurity, or a wish to test their rank. Pay attention when they mock people with less power.

32Status behaviour

Notice whether warmth rises after someone learns a title, school, income, connection, or source of access.

33Skill and confidence

Judge skill by clear details, past results, explanations, predictions, decisions, and how the person corrects mistakes.

34Confidence and control

Someone may control a conversation because they are anxious or worried about rank. A confident person can handle silence, questions, and changing their mind.

35Kindness and agreement

A kind person may disagree. Kindness shows when doing the right thing feels uncomfortable.

36Niceness and trust

Warmth and charm do not prove honesty, care with private information, follow-through, or steady behaviour when the rewards change.

37Intelligence and wisdom

A clever explanation is not the same as sound judgment about what happens next, what it costs, and how it affects people.

38Ambition

Ask what drives the ambition: skill, security, status, power, freedom, impact, praise, revenge, or a better life for the family.

39Loyalty

Loyalty becomes visible when it costs time, money, status, or comfort. It does not require excusing abuse or poor work.

40Integrity

Integrity appears when someone keeps a stated principle while violating it would bring a real advantage.

41Manipulation

Look for repeated guilt, urgency, confusion, isolation, hidden facts, and changing demands that make it harder to choose freely.

42Flattery

Check whether praise is intense, attached to a request, aimed upward, or withdrawn after refusal.

43Too much, too soon

Fast closeness and commitment leave less time to observe. Take more time when more is at risk.

44Romantic interest

Look for several signs together: they start contact too, make time, remember details, open up, make an effort, stay close, and remain engaged.

45Professional interest

Follow-up, clear questions, introductions, useful resources, and planned next steps matter more than polite excitement.

46Negotiators

Before judging personality, track the first offer, what each side gives up, silence, time pressure, other options, limits, and if-then language.

47Leaders

Judge decisions, information flow, incentives, talent growth, ownership, and whether bad news travels safely.

48Teams

Notice who interrupts, gets attention, explains ideas, has expertise, takes blame, protects others, holds the title, and has influence without one.

49Read the room

Observe before joining. Find the person in charge, the person with influence but no title, the connector, the expert, the outsider, the performer, and the quiet influence.

50Who looks at whom?

After uncertainty, conflict, or a risky joke, watch whose reaction the group checks first.

51Seating and distance

Where people sit may suggest comfort, alliance, or rank. Room design and culture make this a weak signal.

52Silence

Silence can mean thought, fear, respect, disagreement, not knowing, or lack of interest. Look at what happens around it.

53Mixed signals

When words, voice, and face do not match, ask why. The cause could be politeness, tiredness, self-control, or resentment.

54Ask about doubts

Name the mismatch without blame: “It sounds like you have some doubts. What concerns you most?”

55Lie detection

No universal gesture proves a lie. Compare accounts, request specifics, verify independently, and track contradictions.

56Update your best guess

Start with several possible causes. Treat each one as more or less likely as you see new behaviour.

57Compare explanations

Write at least three explanations and ask what new evidence would help you tell them apart.

58The three-explanation rule

Write your first reading, a kinder alternative, and an explanation based on a different cause. Then observe.

59Current state and character

A current state belongs to this moment or topic. A part of someone’s character must repeat across different settings.

60Ability and character

Ask three separate questions: Can they deliver? Will they deliver? Is it wise to trust them?

61Intent and impact

Good intent can coexist with repeated harm. Sincerity alone does not make someone safe to rely on.

62Explanation and excuse

An explanation tells you what helped cause the problem. Taking responsibility means the person still owns their part.

63How sure should you be?

Be unsure after one clue, more confident after several clues, and highly confident only when a pattern repeats in different settings.

64Observe without labels

For two weeks, record only what you can see and hear. Do not label emotions or personality.

65Learn what is normal

Learn how familiar people usually speak, make eye contact, joke, sit, use their energy, open up, and ask questions.

66Prediction

Predict one small action before the interaction ends. You can then check whether your feeling was right.

67Prediction journal

Record the situation, prediction, confidence, outcome, and reason for the hit or miss.

68Map their motives

List what the person may want, fear, protect, gain, and change. Keep several answers.

69Ask better questions

Ask what shaped the choice, felt hard, mattered, or could have changed the decision.

70Ask for stories

Ask about a recent decision or disagreement. A story about what someone did tells you more than a broad claim about who they are.

71The last-time question

When someone names a trait, ask when it last appeared under real conditions.

72Watch groups

Meetings and gatherings show who takes the lead, follows, interrupts, repairs harm, gives way, and controls the topic.

73Compare settings

Observe the same person at work, with friends, under stress, after success, with powerful people, and with people who have less power.

74Study your biases

Check whether looks, first impressions, similarity, status, or your existing beliefs are shaping your reading.

75Check through feedback

When it makes sense, ask whether your reading matched their experience. A wrong answer helps you improve.

76The 30-day programme

Move from observation to baselines, prediction, competing explanations, questions, group dynamics, and error review.

77The daily drill

After one interaction, record the facts, what changed from normal, three possible causes, what each person wanted, a prediction, and your confidence.

78Movies as practice

With the sound off, predict each person’s status, relationship, and emotion. Remember that scripted scenes are easier to read than real life.

79Interviews as practice

Pause before each answer. Predict the response, then notice clear details, changes to the story, pushback, and how they talk about failure.

80Negotiations as practice

After meetings, identify what each side needs, what matters to them, their other options, their limits, and their time pressure.

81Children as practice

Observe attention, competition, fairness, sharing, and frustration without assuming children have adult motives.

82Read yourself

Track when you become defensive, seek status, interrupt, hide things, explain too much, or speak differently when anxious.

83Add empathy

Ask which beliefs would make the behaviour make sense. You can understand the cause without agreeing with the action.

84See their point of view

Build the strongest version of what the other person believes, what evidence they have, what they fear, and what they want. Use it to predict their next move.

85Map the rewards

Before blaming personality, map who is involved, their choices, what each choice pays, who knows what, what happens first, and how people can leave.

86A difficult boss

Do not diagnose the boss. Record the patterns you see, offer clear options, put agreements in writing, and rely less on spoken promises.

87A romantic partner

Separate reduced interest, overload, and different communication needs. Predict, then talk directly.

88A business partner

Repeated missed estimates show that their forecasts are unreliable. Stop making plans that depend on hopeful promises.

89Skip the full theory

Answer the question behind your decision: Can they keep a secret, deliver, handle disagreement, respect boundaries, share your goals, do the job, or be trusted with dependence?

90Match depth to the decision

Demand more evidence when more is at risk. Coffee, marriage, hiring, and control of assets need different levels of proof.

91The trust ladder

As someone proves reliable, move from conversation to small promises, responsibility, dependence, and trust where much is at risk.

92Use safeguards

Written agreements, small trials, references, boundaries, and independent checks mean you do not need perfect intuition.

93Use intuition as a lead

Translate “something is off” into an exact cue, a hypothesis, and a test.

94Guard against overreading

Elegant psychological stories deserve more evidence. Sometimes the person was tired.

95Read the larger pattern

Your strongest evidence comes from patterns over time, rewards, trade-offs, pressure, resources, and relationships.

96Add compassion

Ask what the behaviour predicts and what makes it understandable. Combine accuracy, empathy, and boundaries.

97The full sequence

Observe, add the situation and usual behaviour, find what changed, note what each person may gain or lose, form explanations, predict, check, and update.

98Twelve parts to consider

Consider motivation, fear, identity, values, rewards, trust, stress, power, failure, success, relationships, and growth separately.

99Predict behaviour

Understand enough about patterns and likely responses to choose an intelligent action. A total theory is unnecessary.

100Keep track of what you do not know

Observe first. Then find a pattern, form an explanation, make a prediction, check the result, and improve your judgment.

State what you observed. Keep three explanations alive, test their predictions, and match trust to the evidence.
