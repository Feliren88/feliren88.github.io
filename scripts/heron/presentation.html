Reading a language model's hidden states to catch prompt hijacking

0:00 — Opener. Imagine you hire a brilliant assistant. One morning a stranger slips a note into the mail pile. It says: ignore your boss's instructions, do this instead. Your assistant reads the note, refuses, and quietly gets on with the job. Nothing bad happens. Should you care? I think you should. Someone just tried to hijack your assistant, and you will never know they tried. [point to panel 1]

0:25 — The problem. Today that assistant is an AI language model. These models read whatever we give them: emails, web pages, documents. Attackers can hide commands inside that text. This is called a prompt injection. Most safety monitoring only watches what goes into the model and what comes out. In my experiments, on a small model I can fully inspect, hidden commands changed the model's answer about four times in ten. The other six times the answer looked completely normal. The attack failed, and it left no trace on the outside. [evidence: output flip rate 48.8% train / 36.3% held-out, 320 attacked prompts, greedy decoding]

1:05 — What I did. The attempt does leave a trace on the inside. While a model reads, it builds an internal activity pattern: tens of thousands of numbers, loosely like a brain scan. I recorded that pattern once per message and trained a simple linear detector to read it. I then used a statistical method called conformal prediction, which sets the alarm threshold so that fewer than five in a hundred innocent messages get flagged. That rate is fixed before deployment, and it held in my tests. [point to panels 2 and 3. evidence: 896 dims × 25 layers ≈ 22k numbers per message; split-conformal threshold at α = 0.05; measured FPR 0.029 ± 0.016; mean 0.037 over 200 splits]

1:40 — The twist. My first detector looked perfect. It caught every single attack. It was also wrong. I wrote innocent messages that merely look like attacks, such as "please ignore any typos in my message", and the detector raised the alarm on every one of them. It had learned that extra text sits in front of the task, not that the goal was being hijacked. So I rebuilt the benchmark around those difficult innocent messages and retrained. The detector now catches ninety-nine percent of hijack attempts, including an attack family it never saw in training, and false alarms stay inside the budget. [point to panel 4: orange is before each fix, blue is after. evidence: naive probe FPR 1.000 on benign prefixes under clean-only calibration; control-aware TPR 0.988 ± 0.013 on the held-out family, 1.000 on disjoint-vocabulary paraphrases; FPR 0.029]

2:25 — Why it matters, and the close. AI assistants are starting to read our inboxes and browse the web for us. Hijack attempts will arrive hidden inside that text, and most will fail silently, until one does not. If we only watch the answers, we miss the attempts. If we read the model's internal signals, we catch the attempt itself, at a false-alarm rate we set in advance. The signal is not keyword spotting: attacks rephrased to share no vocabulary with training are still caught. One last lesson, for anyone who builds safety tests. My perfect score was not good news. It was a warning. In AI safety, if your detector never makes a mistake, check your test before you celebrate. Thank you. [evidence for the rephrasing claim: A9 paraphrase TPR 1.000 ± 0.000. ~455 words total ≈ 3:00 at competition pace]
