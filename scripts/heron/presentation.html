Imagine you hire a brilliant assistant. A stranger slips a note into the mail pile: ignore your boss, do this instead. Your assistant reads it, refuses, and keeps working. Nothing bad happens. Should you care? Someone just tried to hijack your assistant, and you would never know.
01 · Problem t 0:20

The output hides the attempt

An in-context goal hijack injects an instruction that tries to override the user's task. It changes the model's internal computation whether or not the final output changes. Most deployed monitoring only reads inputs and outputs.

36–49%
of injections actually flip this model's output (greedy decoding, 320 attacked prompts)
≈99%
of hijack attempts are flagged by a linear probe on layer-12 hidden states
Two questions: is there a readable hijack signal inside the model, and can the false-alarm rate be fixed in advance?
Today that assistant is an AI language model reading emails, web pages, and documents attackers can hide commands inside. This is a prompt injection. Most safety monitoring only watches what goes in and what comes out. In my experiments, hidden commands changed the model's answer about four times in ten. The other six times, the attack failed and left no trace on the outside. [evidence: output flip rate 48.8% train / 36.3% held-out, 320 attacked prompts, greedy decoding]
02 · Method t 0:45

One pass, one probe, one calibrated alarm line

Pipeline. Four prompt conditions → one cached forward pass → control-aware probe → conformal threshold on benign calibration scores.
Conformal prediction sets the alarm line so <5% of innocent messages are flagged. The rate is fixed before deployment.
Here is what happens on the inside. One forward pass records the model's internal activity at all 25 layers. A linear probe scores each message. A statistical method called conformal prediction sets the alarm line so that fewer than five in a hundred innocent messages get flagged, and that rate is fixed before deployment. [evidence: split-conformal threshold at α = 0.05; measured FPR 0.029 ± 0.016; mean 0.037 over 200 splits]

05 · Findings t 1:45

The controls changed the conclusion, twice

Detection quality and calibration coverage fail separately. A benchmark that measures only one of them can certify the wrong detector.
Here is the twist. My first detector looked perfect, until I tested it on tricky innocent messages: it flagged every one of them. It had learned that extra text sits before the task, not that the goal was hijacked. Retraining with those messages fixed this: false alarms dropped from 100 percent to under 3 percent. A second issue: an alarm line tuned on one benign format misfired 26 percent of the time on another. Recalibrating brought it back under budget. [evidence: naive probe FPR 1.000 on benign prefixes under clean-only calibration; control-aware FPR 0.029; suffix-position FPR 0.258 → 0.045 after recalibration]
06 · Scale t 2:15

The result holds from 0.5B to 7B

Detection improves with size and false alarms stay near budget. Larger models obeyed the injected command far more often, 27–49% at 0.5B/1.5B and 73–85% at 3B/7B.
Is this a small-model quirk? I reran the whole benchmark on the same model family at four sizes, up to seven billion parameters. Detection got easier as the models grew, and false alarms stayed near budget. One result surprised me. The larger models obeyed the hidden command far more often than the small ones. Skill at following instructions is also skill at following injected ones. The probe still saw nearly every attempt. [evidence: deconf AUC 1.000 ± 0.000 and TPR 0.997–1.000 at 3B/7B; FPR 0.029–0.054 vs α = 0.05; output flip rate 27–49% at 0.5B/1.5B, 73–85% at 3B/7B]
07 · Limits t 2:35

What this does not show

  • Not introspection. An external classifier reads the activations; this is the baseline the project's model-self-use methods must beat.
  • One model family, one attack style. Template-generated injections on Qwen2.5-Instruct (0.5B–7B); semantic hijacks and a second architecture family are the next suite.
  • The guarantee is marginal. It holds on average, and only when real traffic resembles the calibration data. Unfamiliar benign traffic voided the bound until recalibration.
10 ablations · 10 seeds · 5 unit tests, all passing
Three limits. This is an outside probe reading the model, not the model reading itself. It is one family of models and one attack style. The false-alarm guarantee only holds if future traffic looks like my test traffic. It does not transfer automatically.
08 · Take-home t 2:50

Internal telemetry sees the attempt; calibration makes it deployable

A hijack attempt is visible in the residual stream even when the output stays clean, and a split-conformal threshold turns the probe into an alarm with a false-positive budget fixed in advance. A security benchmark needs both confound controls and a coverage-audited calibration set.
