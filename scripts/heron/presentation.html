Imagine you hire a brilliant assistant. A stranger slips a note into the mail pile: ignore your boss, do this instead. Your assistant reads it, refuses, and keeps working. Should you care? Someone just attempted to hijack your assistant, and nothing observable recorded the attempt.
01 · Problem t 0:20

The output hides the attempt

An in-context goal hijack injects an instruction that tries to override the user's task. It changes the model's internal computation whether or not the final output changes. Most deployed monitoring only reads inputs and outputs.

of hidden commands actually change this model's answer (320 attacked prompts)
≈99%
of hijack attempts are flagged by a linear probe (a simple classifier) reading the model's internal activity
Two questions: is there a readable hijack signal inside the model, and can the false-alarm rate be fixed in advance?
For a language model, that note is a prompt injection: adversarial text in the context that tries to override the user's goal. In my experiments, injections flipped the model's output in about four of ten attempts under greedy decoding. The other six leave no behavioral trace, so output monitoring under-fires. [evidence: output flip rate 48.8% train / 36.3% held-out, 320 attacked prompts, greedy decoding]
02 · Method t 0:45

One pass, one probe, one calibrated alarm line

Pipeline. Four kinds of messages go through the model once → a simple classifier (the probe) scores each message from the model's internal activity → the alarm line is set using innocent messages only.
Conformal prediction sets the alarm line so <5% of innocent messages are flagged. The rate is fixed before deployment.
The detector is deliberately simple. One forward pass caches residual-stream activations. A linear probe, logistic regression on one layer's last-token state, scores each prompt. Split conformal prediction sets the threshold on benign calibration scores, with a distribution-free, finite-sample guarantee: at most five percent of benign traffic gets flagged. [evidence: split-conformal threshold at α = 0.05; measured FPR 0.029 ± 0.016; mean 0.037 over 200 splits]

The benchmark is built to catch shortcut learning. Hard negatives reuse the attack templates' command vocabulary in benign senses, like "please ignore any typos in my message". An earlier version without hard negatives saturated at a perfect but uninformative one point zero.

05 · Findings t 1:45

The controls changed the conclusion, twice

Detection quality and calibration coverage fail separately. A benchmark that measures only one of them can certify the wrong detector.
The methodological core. The naive probe reached perfect AUC yet flagged one hundred percent of harmless prefixed prompts: it had learned to detect prefix presence while missing the goal override. Adding the controls to its training class cut false positives to under three percent. Separately, a threshold calibrated on one benign distribution misfired twenty-six percent under distribution shift; recalibration restored validity. [evidence: naive probe FPR 1.000 on benign prefixes under clean-only calibration; control-aware FPR 0.029; suffix-position FPR 0.258 → 0.045 after recalibration]
06 · Scale t 2:15

The result holds from 0.5B to 7B

Detection improves with size and false alarms stay near budget. Larger models obeyed the injected command far more often, 27–49% at 0.5B/1.5B and 73–85% at 3B/7B.
Is this a small-model artifact? I reran the full protocol across the Qwen2.5 family, half a billion to seven billion parameters. Detection improved with scale and false positives stayed near budget. The surprise: injection compliance roughly doubled with scale. Instruction-following capability is also injection-following capability. The probe flagged nearly every attempt regardless. [evidence: deconf AUC 1.000 ± 0.000 and TPR 0.997–1.000 at 3B/7B; FPR 0.029–0.054 vs α = 0.05; output flip rate 27–49% at 0.5B/1.5B, 73–85% at 3B/7B]
07 · Limits t 2:35

What this does not show

  • Not introspection. An external classifier reads the activations; this is the baseline the project's model-self-use methods must beat.
  • One model family, one attack style. Template-generated injections on Qwen2.5-Instruct (0.5B–7B); semantic hijacks and a second architecture family are the next suite.
  • The guarantee is marginal. It holds on average, and only when real traffic resembles the calibration data. Unfamiliar benign traffic voided the bound until recalibration.
  • A plain text filter nearly matches. These attacks are visible in the prompt; a word-counting filter on the input text equals the probe except on unseen attack phrasings (0.88 vs 0.99 caught). Attacks hidden from the input text are the next benchmark.
12 ablations · 10 seeds · 5 unit tests, all passing
Four limits. The method uses an external probe and makes no claim of model introspection. One model family, one attack distribution. The conformal guarantee is marginal and assumes exchangeability with calibration traffic. And these injections are legible in the prompt: a TF-IDF baseline on raw text nearly matches the probe, except on unseen phrasings. Attacks illegible in the input are the decisive next benchmark. [evidence: A12 TF-IDF baseline, held-out-family TPR 0.880 ± 0.063 vs probe 0.988 ± 0.013 at fixed τ; deconfounded AUC 1.000]
08 · Take-home t 2:50

Internal telemetry sees the attempt; calibration makes it deployable

A hijack attempt is visible in the model's internal activity even when the output stays normal, and conformal calibration turns the probe into an alarm whose false-alarm budget is fixed in advance. A security benchmark needs trap messages that look like attacks, a calibration set that covers real traffic, and the cheapest baseline that could replace the detector.
